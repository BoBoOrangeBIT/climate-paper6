# Introduction

<!-- C01 -->
Heat-health and adaptation models represent adaptation almost only as air conditioning. The twentieth-century decline in temperature-related mortality in the United States is attributed mainly to residential air conditioning [@barreca2016adapting; @deschenes2011climate], and higher air-conditioning prevalence coincides with lower heat-related mortality across countries and cities [@sera2020air; @chua2023net]. Global damage functions price adaptation through income and climate-driven responses of the same kind [@carleton2022valuing; @deschenes2022impact], and the cooling modules of integrated assessment models write it as a threshold-and-setpoint rule, namely operation above an outdoor threshold to hold an indoor setpoint [@colelli2020cooling]. The same framing underlies maps of air-conditioning need [@biardeau2020heat] and projections of a cooling gap for households that cannot afford the appliance [@davis2021air; @pavanello2021air; @andrijevic2021future; @falchetta2024inequalities].

<!-- C02 -->
Households in hot regions mostly own something else. About 2.3 billion residential fans were in use in 2016 [@iea2018future], and in India in 2022 households owning only a direct evaporative cooler far outnumbered those owning any air conditioner [@agarwal2026spatializing]. Direct evaporative coolers serve the same role in Iran and Pakistan [@naderi2020simulation; @noor2020evaporative]. A pedestal fan draws roughly one fiftieth of the power of a room air conditioner [@ravanelli2016electric], and a fan-first strategy that switches air conditioning on only above the adaptive comfort limit is estimated to cut residential cooling electricity by about 76 % [@malik2022potential; @dedear2002thermal]. Fan-first cooling is accordingly recommended for low-carbon heat adaptation [@jay2021reducing; @jay2019fanning; @unep2023global; @who2024heat], yet none of these strategies can be evaluated by a model whose only adaptation lever is air conditioning, the most expensive and energy-intensive option.

<!-- C03 -->
The evidence for fans is physiological rather than epidemiological and remains contested. A Cochrane review found no eligible trials [@gupta2012electric]. Biophysical analyses show that fans help above 35 °C in humid air but harm in very dry heat [@jay2015should; @brode2023temperature], and crossover trials found benefit at 40 °C and 51 % relative humidity (RH) but harm at 47 °C and 15 % RH [@morris2019effects; @ravanelli2015heart]. Model-derived upper limits for safe fan use are 39 °C for healthy young adults, 38 °C for older adults and 37 °C for people on anticholinergic medication [@morris2021electric], and another analysis places the limit at 35 °C when RH is below 50 % [@foster2021quantifying]. Trials in older adults found minimal benefit at 38 °C and 60 % RH and a rise in core temperature at 45 °C and 15 % RH [@chaseling2025thermal], and no clinically meaningful change at 36 °C and 45 % RH [@oconnor2024effect]. For direct evaporative coolers the health evidence consists of one chamber study [@rugani2026creating], field comfort surveys in India [@tewari2019field] and one heat-mortality model for India in which they confer less protection than air conditioning [@pavanello2025adaptation].

<!-- C04 -->
A metric that could bridge these literatures already exists in building science. ASHRAE Standard 55 expresses the effect of elevated air speed as a shift of the comfort zone at equal Standard Effective Temperature (SET), the index of the Gagge two-node model, and permits 0.8 m/s without and 1.2 m/s with occupant control [@ashrae2020thermal; @gagge1986standard; @arens2009moving]. On that basis the cooling of moving air has been written as an equivalent reduction in dry-bulb temperature [@schiavon2008energy; @zhang2015review], shown to hold in warm humid conditions [@zhai2013comfort; @zhou2023effects], and used to justify higher setpoints that save cooling energy [@hoyt2015extending]. No study has connected this SET-based metric to the threshold-and-setpoint form through which air conditioning enters exposure–response models, and no comparable metric exists for direct evaporative coolers.

<!-- C05 -->
This letter writes all three technologies in one form, namely that inside an operating window the technology reduces the exposure temperature entering the exposure–response function by an equivalent temperature reduction ΔT that depends on outdoor temperature and humidity. RQ1 asks whether fans and evaporative coolers can be written in the same form as air conditioning without changing the exposure–response function. RQ2 asks how large their equivalent cooling is relative to air conditioning across climates. RQ3 asks how much air-conditioning operation a fan plus air-conditioning combination avoids at no exposure cost. Under the Default air-conditioning rule, 96 % <!-- R:K002 --> of the hours in which air conditioning would run fall inside the 38 °C fan window, a 1.2 m/s fan removes 61 % <!-- R:K003 --> of the exposure degree-hours that air conditioning removes, and raising the air-conditioning threshold by 3 K with a fan avoids 34 % <!-- R:K004 --> of air-conditioning operating hours.
