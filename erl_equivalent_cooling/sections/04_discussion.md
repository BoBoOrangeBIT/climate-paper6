# Discussion

<!-- C18 -->
The main contribution of this letter is a form rather than a number. Heat-health and adaptation models that already carry the air-conditioning rule of Barreca et al [@barreca2016adapting], Carleton et al [@carleton2022valuing] or the integrated assessment cooling module of Colelli and Mistry [@colelli2020cooling] can add electric fans and direct evaporative coolers with two lookup tables on a temperature by humidity grid and two operating windows, while the exposure–response function itself, for example the multi-country curves of Gasparrini et al [@gasparrini2015mortality] or the attribution framework of Vicedo-Cabrera et al [@vicedocabrera2021burden], stays untouched. Because every technology is expressed as the same reduction of the exposure temperature, fan-first and low-energy cooling policies become evaluable in the same machinery that currently prices air conditioning. Technology choice matters for simulated adaptation benefits [@pavanello2025adaptation], and the common form supplies the two technologies that most exposed households actually own.

<!-- C19 -->
Our estimates agree with prior work where the metrics overlap. A fan at 1.2 m/s is worth 3.6 K <!-- R:K001 --> on average over the hours in which air conditioning would run, and the 38 °C window covers 96 % <!-- R:K002 --> of those hours, close to the finding of Morris et al that fan use was beneficial on 96.6 % of hot days for young adults [@morris2021electric]. Tartarini et al report that 0.8 m/s raises the critical operative temperature by up to 14 °C in the same two-node model [@tartarini2021application], a far larger number than ours because it measures the shift of a survivability threshold rather than the equivalent temperature reduction at a comfort setpoint. The fan equivalence ratio of 61 % <!-- R:K003 --> and the 34 % <!-- R:K004 --> of air-conditioning hours avoided by the fan plus air conditioning combination are of the same sign and order as the 76 % reduction in air-conditioning electricity reported by Malik et al for a fan-first strategy [@malik2022potential] and the 29 % cooling energy saving that Hoyt et al simulate for a setpoint extension from 22.2 to 25 °C [@hoyt2015extending], although those studies count energy, which responds nonlinearly to setpoint, whereas ours counts hours and degree-hours. For evaporative coolers, the global equivalence ratio is 72.8 % <!-- R:K051 -->, split into 112 % <!-- R:K054 --> in the dry class and 9 % <!-- R:K055 --> in the humid class. The chamber test of Rugani et al cooled a room by 2.8 K at 32 °C and 15 to 20 % RH while raising RH to 52 % [@rugani2026creating]. Applying our conversion to that measured room state gives an equivalent reduction of only 0.6 K <!-- R:K006 --> at still air, rising to 2.4 K <!-- R:K007 --> at 0.3 m/s and 3.7 K <!-- R:K008 --> at 0.5 m/s, which brackets the manikin cooling effect of about 3 K that the authors report [@rugani2026creating]. The check shows that the humidity penalty in SET is large and that the air movement delivered by the cooler itself is part of its benefit, so the still-air primary estimate is conservative in dry climates.

<!-- C20 -->
Four limitations bound the interpretation. First, the SET index with unlimited sweating never penalises air speed, so only 9.9 % <!-- R:K005 --> of the lookup grid shows a negative fan effect and the operating window had to be imposed from outside. Chamber trials in older adults found fans ineffective at 38 °C and 60 % RH and harmful at 45 °C and 15 % RH [@oconnor2024effect], dehydration worsens the balance [@graham2025electric], and some authors advise older adults to switch fans off in extreme heat [@gagnon2017electric], while the critical review of Meade et al still advises against fans above 35 °C [@meade2024critical]. Letting ΔT turn negative outside the window changes the global equivalence ratio only from 61 % <!-- R:K003 --> to 60.4 % <!-- R:K086 -->, because few air-conditioning hours lie above 38 °C, but a core-temperature definition of ΔT without any window lowers it to 28 % <!-- R:K087 -->, so the choice of physiological criterion matters more than the threshold itself. Second, equivalence is on the SET scale for one seated, lightly clothed adult [@gagge1986standard], SET may be biased in hot-humid air [@li2018modified], and humidity enters only through SET and not through the exposure–response function, whereas large epidemiological studies find little added explanatory power from humidity [@armstrong2019role; @lo2023optimal], a disagreement with physiology that remains unresolved [@baldwin2023humidity; @vanos2023physiological]. Our humid-climate penalty for evaporative coolers should therefore be read as a physiological upper bound. Third, exposure is evaluated at outdoor ERA5 conditions for a single year with land-area weighting, not at indoor temperatures, which are poorly observed and can exceed outdoor values in overheating dwellings [@edwards2025residential; @kenny2024indoor], and the evaporative results depend on ε within the stated range and on the supply humidity cap. Fourth, evaporative coolers consume water, raise indoor humidity in practice [@tewari2019field] and can increase bioaerosol exposure [@johnston2022bioaerosol], and appliance ownership and electricity access are not modelled [@agarwal2026spatializing].

<!-- C21 -->
The results nonetheless carry direct policy relevance. Fan-first guidance in Australia [@bone2025fanfirst] and the WHO advice of 27 °C plus a fan [@who2024heat] can now be scored with the same exposure accounting used for air-conditioning subsidies, and the 3 K allowance it relies on holds across 96 % <!-- R:K002 --> of the hours that matter. Cooling access programmes in hot-dry regions can treat the direct evaporative cooler as at least equivalent to air conditioning under the Default rule, while the same device is close to useless in monsoon and coastal climates, so programme design should follow wet-bulb depression rather than temperature alone [@davis2021air; @andrijevic2021future]. Where models find air-conditioning subsidies cost-effective [@pavanello2025adaptation], the comparison can now include the two cheaper options.
