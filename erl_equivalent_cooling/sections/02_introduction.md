# Introduction

<!-- C01 -->
Heat-health and adaptation models represent adaptation almost only as air conditioning. In the empirical literature, the decline in temperature-related mortality in the United States over the twentieth century is attributed mainly to the diffusion of residential air conditioning [@barreca2016adapting; @deschenes2011climate], and multi-country and city-level studies find that higher air-conditioning prevalence coincides with lower heat-related mortality [@sera2020air; @chua2023net]. Global damage functions price adaptation through income and climate-driven responses of the same kind [@carleton2022valuing; @deschenes2022impact], and the cooling modules of integrated assessment models write it as a threshold-and-setpoint rule, namely the appliance operates once outdoor temperature exceeds a threshold and then holds an indoor setpoint [@colelli2020cooling]. The same framing underlies maps of global air-conditioning need [@biardeau2020heat] and projections of the cooling gap, which show ownership rising steeply with income while a large share of households in hot low-income regions remain unable to afford it [@davis2021air; @pavanello2021air; @andrijevic2021future; @falchetta2024inequalities]. Air conditioning is the most expensive and energy-intensive cooling option, so a model that admits no other technology can only evaluate policies that expand it.

<!-- C02 -->
Households in hot regions mostly own something else. About 2.3 billion residential fans were in use in 2016 [@iea2018future], and in India in 2022 households owning a direct evaporative cooler alone outnumbered those owning any air conditioner by a wide margin [@agarwal2026spatializing]. Direct evaporative coolers serve the same role in the hot dry climates of Iran and Pakistan [@naderi2020simulation; @noor2020evaporative]. The energy difference is large. A pedestal fan draws roughly one fiftieth of the power of a room air conditioner [@ravanelli2016electric], and a fan-first strategy that switches air conditioning on only above the adaptive comfort limit has been estimated to cut residential cooling electricity by about 76 % [@malik2022potential; @dedear2002thermal]. Fan-first cooling is accordingly recommended as a core element of low-carbon heat adaptation [@jay2021reducing; @jay2019fanning; @unep2023global; @who2024heat]. None of these strategies can be evaluated by a model in which the only adaptation lever is air conditioning.

<!-- C03 -->
The health evidence for fans is physiological rather than epidemiological and remains contested. A Cochrane review found no eligible trials [@gupta2012electric]. Biophysical analyses show that fans help above 35 °C in humid air but harm in very dry heat, because convective gain outruns evaporative loss [@jay2015should; @brode2023temperature], and crossover trials found benefit at 40 °C and 51 % RH but harm at 47 °C and 15 % RH [@morris2019effects; @ravanelli2015heart]. Model-derived upper limits for safe fan use are 39 °C for healthy young adults, 38 °C for older adults and 37 °C for people on anticholinergic medication [@morris2021electric], whereas another analysis places the limit at 35 °C when RH is below 50 % [@foster2021quantifying], and a critical review still advises against fans above 35 °C for older people [@meade2024critical]. Trials in older adults found minimal benefit at 38 °C and 60 % RH and a rise in core temperature at 45 °C and 15 % RH [@oconnor2024effect; @chaseling2025thermal], and heat-index based thresholds have also been proposed [@hospers2020electric]. Direct evaporative coolers are nearly absent from the health literature. The available evidence consists of one chamber study of refuge cooling [@rugani2026creating], field comfort surveys in evaporative-cooled Indian offices [@tewari2019field] and one heat-mortality model for India in which evaporative coolers confer less protection than air conditioning [@pavanello2025adaptation].

<!-- C04 -->
A metric that could bridge these literatures already exists in building science. ASHRAE Standard 55 expresses the effect of elevated air speed as a shift of the comfort zone at equal Standard Effective Temperature (SET), the index of the Gagge two-node model, and permits 0.8 m/s without and 1.2 m/s with occupant control [@ashrae2020thermal; @gagge1986standard; @arens2009moving]. On that basis the cooling of moving air has been written as an equivalent reduction in dry-bulb temperature [@schiavon2008energy; @zhang2015review], shown to hold in warm humid conditions [@zhai2013comfort; @zhou2023effects], and used to justify higher setpoints, with a rise from 22.2 to 25 °C saving about 29 % of cooling energy [@hoyt2015extending]. No study has connected this SET-based equivalent temperature reduction to the threshold-and-setpoint form through which air conditioning enters exposure–response models, and no comparable metric exists for direct evaporative coolers. As a result the three technologies cannot be placed on one exposure scale.

<!-- C05 -->
This letter writes all three technologies in the same form, namely that inside an operating window the technology reduces the exposure temperature entering the exposure–response function by an equivalent temperature reduction ΔT that depends on outdoor temperature and humidity. We ask three questions. RQ1 asks whether electric fans and direct evaporative coolers can be written in the same form as air conditioning without changing the exposure–response function. RQ2 asks how large their equivalent cooling is relative to air conditioning across climates. RQ3 asks how much air-conditioning operation a fan plus air-conditioning combination avoids at no exposure cost. The letter contributes a common exposure-reduction form with lookup tables for fans and evaporative coolers that any heat-health model can adopt, a global hourly comparison of the three technologies on ERA5 data for 2020, and a sensitivity analysis over air speed, fan-window thresholds and saturation effectiveness. Under the Default air-conditioning rule, 96 % <!-- R:K002 --> of the hours in which air conditioning would run fall inside the 38 °C fan window, a 1.2 m/s fan removes 61 % <!-- R:K003 --> of the exposure degree-hours that air conditioning removes, and raising the air-conditioning threshold by 3 K with a fan avoids 34 % <!-- R:K004 --> of air-conditioning operating hours.
