# Methods

## A common exposure-reduction form

<!-- C06 -->
We wrote every cooling technology k as an equivalent temperature reduction ΔT_k that acts on the temperature entering the exposure–response function only inside an operating window,

T_exp = T_out − ΔT_k(T_out, RH_out) · 1[window_k],

where T_out and RH_out are outdoor dry-bulb temperature and relative humidity, 1[·] is the indicator function and T_exp is the exposure temperature. This is the form in which air conditioning already enters heat-health and adaptation models, namely operation above a threshold and maintenance of a setpoint [@barreca2016adapting; @carleton2022valuing]. For air conditioning we therefore set ΔT_AC = T_out − T_set whenever T_out > T_set. We used two rules. The Default rule holds 23 °C and 55 % RH. The DLS rule holds 26 °C and 60 % RH, following the decent living standards cooling literature [@kikstra2021decent; @mastrucci2019improving]. The humidity setpoint enters only an SET-consistent variant in which ΔT_AC solves SET(T_out − ΔT, RH_out, still air) = SET(T_set, RH_set, still air), where SET is the Standard Effective Temperature of the Gagge two-node model [@gagge1986standard]. Table 1 defines all four technologies in this form.

## Electric fans

<!-- C07 -->
We defined the fan ΔT with the cooling effect method of ASHRAE 55 [@ashrae2020thermal; @arens2009moving]. ΔT_fan is the dry-bulb reduction at still air (0.1 m/s) and unchanged humidity that yields the same SET as the outdoor state with elevated air speed v_fan, so that ΔT solves SET(T_out − ΔT, RH_out, 0.1 m/s) = SET(T_out, RH_out, v_fan). We computed SET with the two-node model as implemented in pythermalcomfort 4.8 [@tartarini2020pythermalcomfort; @gagge1986standard] for a seated, lightly clothed adult (metabolic rate 1.1 met, clothing 0.5 clo, mean radiant temperature equal to air temperature). The primary air speed was 1.2 m/s, the ASHRAE 55 upper limit with occupant control, with 0.8 m/s (the limit without occupant control) and 2.0 m/s (the speed of chamber trials [@morris2019effects; @oconnor2024effect]) as sensitivity cases. We solved for ΔT by bracketed root finding and allowed negative solutions, so that the lookup table records states in which added air movement raises rather than lowers SET. Equivalent temperatures of this kind have been reported for air movement by earlier building-science studies [@schiavon2008energy; @zhang2015review].

<!-- C08 -->
The SET calculation assumes unlimited sweating, so it never penalises air speed strongly. Only 9.9 % <!-- R:K005 --> of cells on the temperature by humidity grid have a negative cooling effect at 1.2 m/s, all of them hot and very dry, whereas physiological trials show fans increasing heat strain at lower temperatures [@oconnor2024effect; @wang2022biophysical; @brode2023temperature]. We therefore imposed an external operating window taken from the published fan safety envelopes. The primary window is T_out < 38 °C, the threshold derived for healthy older adults by Morris et al [@morris2021electric]. Sensitivity windows were 39 °C (healthy young adults) and 37 °C (adults on anticholinergic medication) from the same study, 35 °C from older public guidance [@meade2024critical], and the rule of Foster et al that fans are ineffective at or above 35 °C when RH is below 50 % [@foster2021quantifying]. Outside the window ΔT_fan = 0 in the main analysis, meaning the fan is switched off and the exposure benefit is lost but no harm is counted. In a further sensitivity test we let ΔT_fan take negative values outside the window, derived from the two-node core temperature with sweating limits as implemented in the use_fans_heatwaves model [@tartarini2021application].

## Direct evaporative coolers

<!-- C09 -->
For a direct evaporative cooler we first computed the supply air state. The supply dry-bulb temperature is T_s = T_out − ε (T_out − T_wb), where T_wb is the outdoor wet-bulb temperature and ε is the saturation effectiveness [@duan2012indirect]. The supply humidity ratio follows the adiabatic saturation line, which fixes the supply relative humidity RH_s. We used ε = 0.75 as the primary value and 0.6 and 0.85 as bounds, a range consistent with measured pad performance [@camargo2005experimental; @harby2019investigation] and with the 65 to 80 % efficiency assumed in a recent heat-mortality study [@lo2023optimal]. We then converted the supply state to the common scale by solving SET(T_out − ΔT, RH_out, 0.1 m/s) = SET(T_s, RH_s, 0.1 m/s), so that ΔT_DEC is the dry-bulb reduction at outdoor humidity and still air that is SET-equivalent to breathing the humid supply air. The operating window requires ΔT_DEC > 0 and RH_s ≤ 80 %, with 70 % and no cap as sensitivity cases, together with a variant that excludes hours with T_wb > 24 °C and a variant in which the occupant sits in the 0.5 m/s supply airstream. The humidity cap reflects the moisture and bioaerosol burden of evaporative cooling in humid air [@johnston2022bioaerosol; @tewari2019field].

<!-- C09 -->
We checked the conversion step against the chamber measurements of Rugani et al [@rugani2026creating], who operated a direct evaporative cooler at 32 °C and 15 to 20 % RH and recorded a room cooling of 2.8 K, a rise in room RH to 52 % and a manikin cooling effect of about 3 K. Applying our SET conversion to the measured room state at 17.5 % outdoor RH gives an equivalent reduction of 0.6 K <!-- R:K006 --> at still air, 2.4 K <!-- R:K007 --> at 0.3 m/s and 3.7 K <!-- R:K008 --> at 0.5 m/s, which brackets the manikin value once the air movement of the supply jet is included. The supply-state model itself predicts a much larger dry-bulb drop of 11.6 K <!-- R:K009 --> for that chamber because it describes the air leaving the pad rather than the mixed room, so we report it as an upper bound for an occupant in the supply airstream.

## Fan plus air conditioning

<!-- C10 -->
We represented the combination as air conditioning whose threshold and setpoint are both raised by 3 K, the comfort-zone allowance that ASHRAE 55 grants for elevated air speed at 1.2 m/s [@ashrae2020thermal; @hoyt2015extending; @zhou2023effects] and the shift observed in fan-first field studies [@malik2022potential]. The fan runs inside its window whenever T_out exceeds the original setpoint, and air conditioning holds T_set + 3 K with the fan still running above the raised threshold. At the raised Default setpoint the fan lookup gives 3.4 K <!-- R:K076 -->, so the exposure benefit inside the fan window is by construction at least that of air conditioning alone, while air-conditioning operating hours and cooling degree-hours fall. We report the benefit ratio relative to air conditioning alone to make the small surplus explicit.

## Data, lookup tables and metrics

<!-- C11 -->
We applied the four rules hour by hour to ERA5 reanalysis [@hersbach2020era5] 2 m temperature and 2 m dew point for 2020 on the native reduced Gaussian N320 grid of about 31 km, which has 542 080 <!-- R:K103 --> points globally, of which 157 163 <!-- R:K102 --> are land points under the ERA5 land-sea mask (fraction at least 0.5). We converted dew point to RH with the Magnus formula and the coefficients of Alduchov and Eskridge [@alduchov1996improved], and computed the wet-bulb temperature with the psychrometric functions of pythermalcomfort [@tartarini2020pythermalcomfort] [[AUTHOR TO CONFIRM: pythermalcomfort psychrometric routine versus the Stull 2011 formula]]. Because the SET root finding is slow, we precomputed ΔT and the windows for every technology on a grid from 20 to 50 °C in 0.25 K steps and from 5 to 100 % RH in 1 % steps and applied bilinear interpolation in the hourly pass. For each rule r and technology k we accumulated the following annual metrics. AC hours are hours with T_out > T_set(r). Exposure degree-hours removed are Σ_h ΔT_k(h)·1[window_k(h)] over the AC hours of the rule, in K h per year. The equivalence ratio is the exposure degree-hours removed by k divided by those removed by air conditioning under the same rule. Coverage is the share of AC hours inside the window of k. AC hours avoided under fan plus air conditioning are 1 − hours(T_out > T_set + 3 K)/hours(T_out > T_set), with the analogous ratio for cooling degree-hours. Global values are area-weighted means over land points, with weights cos(lat)/n_row, and maps and distributions are restricted to land points with at least 100 AC hours per year and aggregated to 0.5°. We classified land points by the share of hours above 23 °C that have T_wb > 24 °C, namely dry below 10 %, intermediate 10 to 50 % and humid 50 % or more. We extracted hourly series for twelve cities spanning hot-dry (Phoenix, Riyadh, Cairo, Tehran), monsoon (Delhi, Karachi), hot-humid (Guangzhou, Singapore, Lagos, Miami) and temperate (Beijing, Paris) climates. All exposures are evaluated at outdoor conditions and all area statistics are land-area based, not population weighted. The code and the lookup tables are released with the paper.
